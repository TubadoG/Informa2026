# Early dropout detection in clinical trials: peer-reviewed literature notes

Scope: predictors of participant dropout (withdrawal, LTFU, discontinuation), published predictive models / early-warning approaches, dispensing- or adherence-based signals, sample-size and generalizability limits, and the Tufts CSDD headline figures. Compiled 2026-09-20 for a conference talk aimed at clinical supply / IRT professionals. Access note: several publisher pages (medRxiv, ScienceDirect, Wiley, AACR, Springer) blocked fetches, so some entries rely on abstracts, PMC mirrors, or secondary summaries; these are flagged.

Evidence-strength legend used below: **[Validated]** = peer-reviewed with held-out or external validation; **[Internal only]** = peer-reviewed but cross-validation / single split only; **[Descriptive]** = association study, no predictive model; **[Vendor / grey]** = conference abstract, vendor publication, or industry report, not independently replicated.

---

## Key question 1: What are the empirically supported predictors of dropout?

### Takeaway
The most consistently replicated predictors are (a) trial/protocol burden (duration, procedure count, visit load, travel), (b) adverse events and lack of perceived benefit (including placebo assignment), (c) early behavioural signals inside the trial (missed or rescheduled visits, incomplete procedures, non-adherence), and (d) a small set of baseline patient/site factors that vary by therapeutic area (age, anxiety, caregiver type, site staffing, clinician referral). Most of this evidence is descriptive or single-trial; very little is pooled across sponsors with individual-level data.

### Cited Findings

**Industry-level dropout figures (Tufts CSDD)**
- Tufts CSDD Impact Report data (as reported by Ken Getz): the average dropout rate across all studies and therapeutic areas rose from 15.3% (2012) to 19.1% (2019), a 25% increase over seven years, in late-phase trials globally. CNS trials rose 19.2% to 25.9%; oncology 18.2% to 19.3%; vaccines 12.3% in 2019; cardiovascular 7% and rare disease 6.5% fared best. — [Getz, "Can Recruitment and Retention Get Any Worse?", Applied Clinical Trials Online](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/can-recruitment-and-retention-get-any-worse) (page blocked fetch; figures confirmed via secondary summary at [Replior blog quoting the Tufts CSDD Impact Report](https://www.replior.com/resources/all/blog/the-dreaded-patient-drop-out/)). The same ACT article also states 81% of patients screened for rare-disease trials are ineligible and 56% of rare-disease volunteers fail to randomize. **[Descriptive / industry report]**
- Separate Tufts CSDD figure, 2010 to 2020: dropout rates increased 105.1% (a relative increase, not an absolute rate), alongside a 27.2% increase in protocol-approval-to-FPI duration, 36.9% increase in enrollment duration, and 113.3% increase in substantial amendments. — [Clinical Trial Vanguard coverage of Tufts CSDD presentation](https://www.clinicaltrialvanguard.com/conference-coverage/tufts-csdd-new-insights-on-the-clinical-trial-industry/). Note the two Tufts figures are from different analyses and denominators; do not put them on the same slide as if one series.
- Tufts CSDD / Medable DCT analysis: in Phase 3 trials using wearables and mobile apps, dropout "decreased from 28.6% to 26.1%" and screen-failure rates fell about 10%. — [Clinical Leader, "Tufts Finds Substantial Net Benefits To Using Decentralized Trials" (2022)](https://www.clinicalleader.com/doc/tufts-finds-substantial-net-benefits-to-using-decentralized-trials-0001). **[Vendor-supported industry report]**; the 28.6% baseline is a different population from the 19.1% figure above.

**Protocol burden and trial design**
- Smith Z, Bilke S, Pretorius S, Getz K et al. (2021), "Protocol Design Variables Highly Correlated with, and Predictive of, Clinical Trial Performance", Therapeutic Innovation & Regulatory Science, DOI 10.1007/s43441-021-00370-0. Reports protocol design variables (procedures, eligibility criteria, endpoints) correlating with performance metrics including dropout. — [Springer link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43441-021-00370-0) (full text blocked; effect sizes not captured). **[Descriptive]**
- Tufts CSDD (Getz) trend work: a steady increase in procedures per protocol, eligibility criteria and CRF pages over the past decade, producing longer trials and worsening recruitment/retention. — [PharmaTimes summary of Tufts study](https://pharmatimes.com/news/protocol_complexity_is_increasing_trial_burden,_tufts_study_says_985956/); [Applied Clinical Trials, "The Heavy Burden of Protocol Design"](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/heavy-burden-protocol-design). **[Descriptive]**
- Hutchison ER, Zhang YY, Nampally S, Neelufer IK, Malkov V, Weatherall J, Khan F, Shameer K (AstraZeneca), "Modeling Clinical Trial Attrition Using Machine Intelligence: a driver analytics case study using 1,325 trials representing one million patients", medRxiv 2021, DOI 10.1101/2021.11.12.21266277. Using ClinicalTrials.gov/AACT data (respiratory domain; 3,175 cohorts, 1,020,085 patients, 79 curated features), the top features driving cohort-level attrition were Duration of Trial, Duration of Treatment, Indication, and Number of Adverse Events. — [Semantic Scholar record of the preprint](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1101/2021.11.12.21266277?fields=title,authors,year,abstract,venue); code at [AstraZeneca/CTELC-Patient-Attrition-Model on GitHub](https://github.com/AstraZeneca/CTELC-Patient-Attrition-Model). **[Preprint, not peer-reviewed as of retrieval]**

**Adverse events, efficacy, and placebo**
- Antipsychotic trials, IPD meta-analysis (2026): original studies classified dropouts as inefficacy 20.1% of randomized participants, adverse events 6.5%, and other reasons including consent withdrawal 17.2%; the authors note reasons are inconsistently reported and AE-driven dropouts are under-captured. — [Reclassifying dropouts from antipsychotic trials due to study-reported adverse events, European Neuropsychopharmacology 2026, PubMed 42139983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42139983/). **[Meta-analysis]**
- Kemmler G et al. (2005), "Dropout rates in placebo-controlled and active-control clinical trials of antipsychotic drugs: a meta-analysis", Archives of General Psychiatry (now JAMA Psychiatry). Active-arm dropout was 48.1% in placebo-controlled vs 28.3% in active-control trials; within placebo-controlled trials placebo-arm dropout was 60.2% vs 48.1% for the active arm. — [PubMed 16330718](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16330718/); [JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/209094). **[Meta-analysis, seminal]**
- Furukawa TA et al. (2018), "Impact of placebo arms on outcomes in antidepressant trials: systematic review and meta-regression analysis", International Journal of Epidemiology 47(5):1454-1464. All-cause dropout risk rose about 4% for every 10% increase in the probability of being randomized to placebo. — [IJE / Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/47/5/1454/5034083); [PMC6208275](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6208275). **[Meta-analysis]**
- Placebo-arm (nocebo) dropouts in oral anti-osteoporotic drug RCTs: 8% on average across 16,460 placebo-treated patients, higher in older patients. — [PubMed 36596944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36596944/). **[Meta-analysis]**
- PTSD pharmacotherapy trials: Wright SL, Kessler A et al. (2025), European Journal of Psychotraumatology, DOI 10.1080/20008066.2025.2504839. 43 RCTs / 4,829 participants: dropout 27% drug vs 30% placebo (RR 0.92, p = 0.099, not significant); IPD from 3 trials (n = 246) found no significant effect of age, marital status, PTSD severity or comorbidity; male sex had 1.96x higher dropout in the drug arm only, which disappeared after adjustment. — [PMC12120863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12120863/). **[Meta-analysis; useful as a negative result on demographics]**
- Oncology (CRC survey): main reasons for withdrawal in cancer trials were disease progression 71.5%, adverse events 10.6%, consent withdrawal for personal reasons 5.5%. — [Participation in and withdrawal from cancer clinical trials: a survey of clinical research coordinators, PMC9096735](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9096735/). **[Survey]**

**Consent comprehension, burden, and site factors (narrative reviews)**
- Fogel DB (2018), "Factors associated with clinical trials that fail and opportunities for improving the likelihood of success: a review", Contemporary Clinical Trials Communications 11:156-164, PubMed 30112460. Cited findings: patients who dropped out early were twice as likely to report difficulty understanding the consent form (35% vs 16% among completers); side effects, extra examinations, uncertainty of benefit and financial impact are common concerns; site experience, staff enthusiasm/engagement and dedicated support staff are associated with better retention. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30112460/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451865418300693). **[Narrative review; the 35% vs 16% figure is a secondary citation inside Fogel]**
- Kadam RA, Borde SU, Madas SA, Salvi SS, Limaye SS (2016), "Challenges in recruitment and retention of clinical trial subjects", Perspectives in Clinical Research 7(3):137-143, DOI 10.4103/2229-3485.184820. Survey of 73 Indian investigators: serious adverse events, fear of study procedures (47%) and side effects (44%) were rated as moderately affecting retention. — [PubMed 27453831](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27453831/). **[Investigator opinion survey, not outcome data]**

**In-trial behavioural signals: missed visits and non-adherence**
- Wheeler AJ, Garg H, Kaushik D, Mansour A, Pruthi D, Liss MA (2022), "Predictors of participants' retention: socioeconomic factors or nonadherence", Trials, DOI 10.1186/s13063-022-06901-w. Prospective urology study, n = 123, 13.8% withdrew. Non-adherence (any incomplete study procedure or scheduling issue): 100% of withdrawn vs 48.1% of retained had at least one event; repeated non-adherence 88.2% vs 16.9%; number of non-adherences was the only independent predictor, OR 12.5 (95% CI 3.5-43.1). Socioeconomic (DCI), age, race, BMI, comorbidity were not predictive. — [PMC9716754](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9716754/). **[Descriptive, single site, 17 events; strong signal but wide CI]**
- Look AHEAD (Fitzpatrick SL et al. 2014, Obesity, DOI 10.1002/oby.20613): age, marital status, depressive symptoms and frequency of self-weighing were associated with missed consecutive data-collection visits, which the authors treat as a proxy for high dropout risk. — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/oby.20613) (full text blocked; effect sizes not captured). **[Descriptive]**
- Predictors of missed research appointments in a randomized placebo-controlled trial (2014) — [PubMed 25386581](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25386581/) (abstract not retrievable due to cookie wall; listed for follow-up).

**Baseline patient and site factors (therapeutic-area specific)**
- A4 preclinical Alzheimer's trial: Raman R, Hussen K, Donohue MC et al. (2025), Alzheimer's & Dementia, DOI 10.1002/alz.093124. n = 1,169 randomized, 339 (29%) discontinued over median 759 days. Multivariable predictors: baseline anxiety (STAI) OR 1.07 per point (95% CI 1.02-1.12) and age OR 1.06 per year (1.03-1.09); sex, race/ethnicity, cognition and amyloid/tau PET were not associated. No AUC reported. — [PMC11712999](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11712999/). **[Descriptive, single large trial]**
- Pooled Phase 3 AD trials (Medidata/AbbVie, AAIC 2021): Crimin K, Allen PJ et al., "Identifying predictive factors of patient dropout in Alzheimer's disease clinical trials", Alzheimer's & Dementia 2021, DOI 10.1002/alz.052361. Factors: age, race, certain medical histories and adverse events (e.g. anxiety), friend as caregiver, low surrounding population density, and clinical staff size at the site. — [Wiley abstract](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/alz.052361); [Medidata blog summary](https://www.medidata.com/en/life-science-resources/medidata-blog/alzheimers-disease-clinical-trials-patient-dropout/). **[Conference abstract / vendor]**
- Early-phase oncology: ECOG performance status, creatinine clearance, alkaline phosphatase, AST, haemoglobin, albumin and platelets predict early treatment discontinuation on Phase I trials. — [Predictors of early treatment discontinuation in Phase I oncology trials, Oncotarget 2015, PMC4662493](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4662493/); [Clinical predictors of early trial discontinuation in Phase I oncology, PMC8151105](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8151105/). **[Descriptive]** Note: in oncology "early discontinuation" is dominated by disease progression, a clinical outcome, not disengagement.
- Remote digital studies: Pratap A, Neto EC, Snyder P et al. (2020), "Indicators of retention in remote digital health studies: a cross-study evaluation of 100,000 participants", npj Digital Medicine 3:21, DOI 10.1038/s41746-020-0224-8. Eight studies 2014-2019, >100,000 participants, ~3.5 million remote assessments; median retention 5.5 days (range 2-26 days across studies). Four factors associated with better retention: clinician referral, older age, compensation, and having the clinical condition. — [PubMed 32128451](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32128451/); [PMC7026051](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7026051/). **[Descriptive, large; app studies, not IMP trials]**
- Data-driven analysis of two prospective digital-health cohorts (Lancet Digital Health 2024): key factors for adherence and retention identified by machine-learning approaches. — [Lancet Digital Health, PIIS2589-7500(24)00219-X](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(24)00219-X/fulltext) (full text blocked; details not captured).

**Travel distance**
- Cancer trial participants: median one-way distance 25.8 miles; patients from lower-income areas travelled 58.3 vs 17.8 miles. — [At What Cost to Clinical Trial Enrollment? A retrospective study of patient travel burden in cancer clinical trials, PubMed 29700209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29700209/). **[Descriptive; travel burden measured, dropout link not tested in this paper]**
- Claims that reduced travel cut dropout to 9% vs a "30% industry standard" (ophthalmic, point-of-need delivery) come from a service vendor. — [2020 On-site](https://www.2020onsite.com/eyes-on-research/how-to-reduce-patient-travel-burden-in-clinical-trials-by-82). **[Vendor / grey; do not cite as evidence]**

**Retention interventions (what actually moves the needle)**
- Gillies K, Kearney A, Keenan C, Treweek S, Hudson J, Brueton VC et al. (2021), "Strategies to improve retention in randomised trials", Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Issue 3, DOI 10.1002/14651858.MR000032.pub3, PubMed 33675536. Searches to January 2020. Conclusion: some approaches may improve postal-questionnaire return rates; evidence is low quality and the effect of most retention strategies "is still not clear". — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.MR000032.pub3/full/po); [Cochrane blog](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/blog-retention-clinical-trials-how-can-we-keep-participants-involved). **[Systematic review; the honest framing for "and then what do you do about it"]**

### Inferences
- The strongest, most transferable predictors for an operational early-warning system are behavioural and observable in operational systems: missed/rescheduled visits, incomplete procedures, and adverse events. Baseline demographics are weak and inconsistent across therapeutic areas (A4, PTSD IPD, urology all found little from demographics).
- Placebo assignment is a genuine risk factor at the arm level, but for a blinded trial it is not usable as a per-patient flag; it argues for planning higher overage/retention effort at trial level.
- Therapeutic area strongly conditions both base rate (6.5% rare disease to 25.9% CNS) and predictor set, so any "universal" flag list should be presented as a starting hypothesis, not a validated scorecard.

### Gaps
- No pooled, sponsor-independent, participant-level meta-analysis of dropout predictors across therapeutic areas was found; the AstraZeneca work is cohort-level and respiratory-only.
- Distance-to-site as a quantified dropout predictor (hazard ratio) in IMP trials was not found in the peer-reviewed sources retrieved; only burden descriptions and vendor claims.
- Effect sizes for protocol design variables vs dropout (Smith 2021, TIRS) could not be extracted because the full text was blocked.

## Key question 2: Published predictive models / early-warning approaches

### Takeaway
Published participant-level dropout models exist in AD, oncology, T1D, HIV care and digital studies, with reported discrimination mostly in the AUC 0.65-0.80 range; nearly all are single-dataset with internal validation only, several are conference abstracts or vendor publications, and the best-calibrated, most transparent example (NSABP B-35) is a dynamic Cox model on PRO data with AUC ~0.76-0.78 and wide confidence intervals. Trial-level (not participant-level) attrition models on ClinicalTrials.gov data reach AUROC ~0.73 but do not help operations inside a running trial.

### Cited Findings

**Participant-level models inside interventional trials**
- **NSABP B-35 (oncology, PROs, dynamic)**: "Dynamic Risk Prediction of Treatment Discontinuation Using Patient-Reported Outcomes Data in the Phase III NSABP B-35 Trial", Cancer Prevention Research 16(11):631, 2023, DOI 10.1158/1940-6207.CAPR-23-0216. Partly conditional Cox models with time-dependent PRO covariates; 70/30 train/validation split; 6-month prediction horizon. Anastrozole arm: baseline BMI plus longitudinal insomnia, joint pain, hot flashes, headaches, gynecologic symptoms and vaginal discharge, Brier 0.039, AUC 0.76 (95% CI 0.57-0.95). Tamoxifen arm: BMI plus cognitive problems, happiness, calmness, weight problems, pain; Brier 0.032, AUC 0.78 (95% CI 0.65-0.91). Deployed as a Shiny calculator. — [PMC10618646](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618646/); [AACR](https://doi.org/10.1158/1940-6207.CAPR-23-0216). Companion JCO paper on toxicity index and PROs predicting early discontinuation in B-35: [JCO 2021, DOI 10.1200/JCO.21.00910](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.00910). **[Internal only; note the CI lower bound of 0.57 is near chance]**
- **OncoBERT (oncology, NLP on clinical notes)**: Olgiati L, Filori Q, Toueress S, Brutti-Mairesse C, Zidane L, Ikhlef N, Gueguen L, Verlingue L (2026), "Automatic prediction of patients early discontinuation in oncology clinical trials", ESMO Real World Data and Digital Oncology, DOI 10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728. 9,039 patients across 1,234 trials at Centre Léon Bérard (2000-2023); early discontinuation defined as ≤60 days from consent to end of participation. Retrospective test (1,007 reports): precision 0.77, recall 0.95, AUROC 0.69; prospective Phase I board (163 patients): precision 0.75, recall 0.75, AUROC 0.67. Poor calibration (Brier 0.20); 84% of ED cases were still predicted as continuing. — [PMC13427660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13427660/). **[Validated prospectively, but modest discrimination; dominated by disease progression]**
- **Alzheimer's Phase 3 pooled (Medidata/AbbVie)**: Crimin K, Allen PJ et al. (2021), Alzheimer's & Dementia, DOI 10.1002/alz.052361. Pooled cross-industry Phase 3 AD trials from Medidata's Enterprise Data Store (Rave EDC-derived); models predicted dropout at 3, 6 and 12 months with ROC-AUC ≥ 0.60 but PR-AUC < 0.24 and systematic overestimation of dropout probability (many false positives). — [Wiley abstract](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/alz.052361); [Medidata blog](https://www.medidata.com/en/life-science-resources/medidata-blog/alzheimers-disease-clinical-trials-patient-dropout/). **[Conference abstract; the most honest published statement that EDC-based dropout prediction is only modestly better than chance]**
- **Type 1 diabetes (Cognivia Compl-AI)**: Ooghe A, Van Rampelbergh J, Branders S, Xaborov N, Paul J, Demolle D, Pereira A, "Predicting drop-out in early-stage T1D clinical trials", ATTD 2025 (19 March 2025). IMPACT study plus substudy, n = 109 (85 + 24); multivariate Cox on baseline engagement questionnaire (site perception, belief in medicine, health literacy); C-index 0.82, ROC-AUC 0.80, sensitivity 93% at 65% specificity; Monte-Carlo cross-validation; outcome = consent withdrawal or non-adherence. — [Cognivia publication page](https://cognivia.com/publication/predicting-drop-out-in-early-stage-type-1-diabetes-clinical-trials-to-improve-retention-through-personalized-engagement-strategies/); [Clinical Trials Arena coverage](https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/cognivias-ai-software-predicts-probability-of-trial-patient-dropping-out/). **[Vendor conference abstract; n = 109, internal CV only; treat as promising, not validated]**
- **A4 (AD) baseline predictors**: Raman et al. 2025 (above), n = 1,169, logistic regression, no AUC reported. — [PMC11712999](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11712999/). **[Descriptive]**

**Loss-to-follow-up models in HIV care (EHR, not RCTs, but the closest analogue with dispensing data)**
- Ridgway JP, Mason JA, Friedman EE et al. (2025), "Comparison of machine learning models to predict loss to follow-up among people with HIV", JAMIA Open 8(4):ooaf077, DOI 10.1093/jamiaopen/ooaf077. 7,340 patients / 65,167 encounters, single FQHC network in Chicago; LTFU = ≥365 days between visits (~13% prevalence). Random forest on structured EMR + bag-of-words NLP: AUC 0.787 (95% CI 0.776-0.798), sensitivity 84.8%, specificity 57.5%; structured only 0.753; NLP only 0.624. Top structured features: antiretroviral regimen, insurance status, mental-health diagnoses. 70/30 split, single centre. — [PMC12289141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12289141/). **[Internal only]**
- Haiti national EMR cohort (2026): CatBoost with class weighting, ROC-AUC up to 0.896, recall 0.773, F2 0.607 in one quarter; strongest features were prior visit and viral-load test frequency, ART dispensation patterns, and facility location. — [International Journal of Public Health 2026, DOI 10.3389/ijph.2026.1609496](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.3389/ijph.2026.1609496/full); [PubMed 42131292](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42131292/). **[Internal only; important because ART dispensing frequency was a top feature]**
- Ethiopia routine-EMR LTFU model (six classifiers) — [BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making 2025, DOI 10.1186/s12911-025-03030-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12911-025-03030-7); systematic review of ML for HIV treatment interruption — [PMC12288308](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12288308/). **[Reviews note heterogeneous validity and limited applicability]**

**Trial-level attrition models (public registry data)**
- Hutchison et al. 2021 (AstraZeneca, medRxiv): cohort-level attrition regression on 3,175 respiratory cohorts, random forest, test-set RMSE 6.64 percentage points (n = 637 cohorts). — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1101/2021.11.12.21266277?fields=title,authors,year,abstract,venue). **[Preprint; predicts a trial's overall dropout %, not which patient]**
- TrialBench (Chen J et al. 2024, arXiv 2407.00631; accepted Nature Scientific Data 2025): "patient dropout" task on 62,058 ClinicalTrials.gov trials with reported dropout counts; multimodal DNN baselines reach ROC-AUC 0.73 (Phase II) and 0.73 (Phase III) for high/low dropout classification, with regression R² 0.63 (Phase I) and 0.40 (Phase II). Authors flag selection bias toward trials with complete results reporting. — [arXiv 2407.00631v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00631v2); [Scientific Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-025-05680-8). **[Benchmark; trial-level only]**
- Related: interpretable ML for early trial termination (Scientific Reports 2023) — [PMC9813129](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9813129/); recruitment forecasting with dropout (Poisson-gamma), arXiv 2202.06779 — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06779). These are planning tools, not patient early-warning.

**Statistical (non-ML) dynamic approaches from the missing-data literature**
- Joint models of longitudinal outcome plus time-to-dropout provide dynamic, individual dropout-risk updates; an extended joint model with an autoregressive submodel "outperformed a baseline-only survival model in predicting dropout" in intensive longitudinal (EMA) data. — [Predicting dropout in intensive longitudinal data: extending the joint model for autocorrelated data, PubMed 41129394](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41129394/); [Rizopoulos page](https://www.drizopoulos.com/publication/apa_jmdropout/). — Bayesian joint model with intermittent missingness and dropout for HIV prevention trials: Wu et al. 2019, Statistics in Medicine, DOI 10.1002/sim.8379 — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sim.8379). **[Methodological; performance metrics not standardized as AUC]**

**Comparative ML in adjacent (non-trial) dropout settings**
- Outpatient alcohol-use-disorder dropout: AdaBoost AUROC 0.72 across six model families. — [PMC8328309](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8328309/). Alcohol recovery programme dropout ML — [PMC11355543](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11355543/). **[Non-trial; shows the typical 0.7 ceiling with behavioural/clinical tabular data]**

### Inferences
- Across settings and data types, participant-level dropout discrimination clusters at AUC 0.65-0.80; anything reported above 0.85 in this literature comes from either an EHR population with extreme class imbalance handling (Haiti) or a very small vendor sample (n = 109). A talk should present 0.7 to 0.8 as the realistic ceiling with today's data.
- Precision is the recurring failure mode (Medidata PR-AUC < 0.24; OncoBERT 84% of ED cases missed at the operating point used; JAMIA Open specificity 57.5%). Early-warning flags will generate many false alarms unless the base rate is high.
- The models that report calibration (NSABP B-35 Brier 0.03-0.04; OncoBERT Brier 0.20) show it is achievable with simple Cox models on repeated PRO data and poor with black-box NLP; for supply/IRT audiences, the transparent Cox/logistic approach is the defensible reference.
- No published model uses IRT/RTSM, CTMS, or supply-chain data as inputs; the data sources actually used are EDC (Medidata), PROs, EHR/clinical notes, registry metadata, and questionnaires.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, externally validated participant-level dropout model in a multi-sponsor interventional trial dataset was found.
- Lead time ("how many weeks before withdrawal did the flag fire") is almost never reported; NSABP B-35 uses a 6-month horizon, Medidata 3/6/12 months, OncoBERT ≤60 days from consent. No paper reports median lead time between flag and actual withdrawal.
- No CNS (non-AD) or diabetes (beyond the n = 109 T1D abstract) participant-level model with reported AUC was found in the retrieved sources.

## Key question 3: Dispensing, medication return, pill-count or adherence data as an early withdrawal signal

### Takeaway
There is no peer-reviewed study found that uses IRT/RTSM dispensing events, medication-return counts, or supply-chain data as the input to a dropout prediction model in an interventional trial. The nearest evidence is indirect: HIV-care models where ART dispensation frequency is a top LTFU feature, a small urology study where in-trial non-adherence events predicted withdrawal (OR 12.5), and the adherence-measurement literature showing pill counts are inflated and least reliable for the very participants who are struggling.

### Cited Findings
- ART dispensation patterns, prior visit frequency and viral-load test frequency were the strongest features for predicting HIV LTFU in Haiti's national EMR (CatBoost, ROC-AUC up to 0.896). — [International Journal of Public Health 2026](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.3389/ijph.2026.1609496/full). **[Internal only; routine care, not RCT]**
- In-trial non-adherence events (incomplete procedures, scheduling problems) predicted withdrawal with OR 12.5 (95% CI 3.5-43.1); every withdrawn participant had at least one prior non-adherence event. — [Wheeler et al. 2022, Trials, PMC9716754](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9716754/). **[Descriptive, n = 123]**
- Cognivia's T1D model explicitly defines the outcome as dropout "due to informed consent withdrawal or non-adherence", i.e. adherence failure is treated as part of the outcome, not a predictor. — [Cognivia](https://cognivia.com/publication/predicting-drop-out-in-early-stage-type-1-diabetes-clinical-trials-to-improve-retention-through-personalized-engagement-strategies/). **[Vendor abstract]**
- Pill counts systematically over-estimate adherence in trials and are unreliable as a measure; electronic monitoring (MEMS) is the reference standard with >700 publications, and can distinguish initiation, implementation and discontinuation phases. — [El Alili et al. 2016, scoping review of MEMS vs alternative methods, Br J Clin Pharmacol, DOI 10.1111/bcp.12942, PMC4917812](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4917812/); [Summary measures of adherence using pill counts in two HIV prevention trials, PMC3812335](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3812335/); [Vrijens (AARDEX), industry commentary on pill counts](https://aardexgroup.com/pill-count-compliance-its-time-to-burst-the-drug-trial-bubble/). **[Measurement literature; the last is vendor commentary]**
- Combined pill count plus self-report predicts ART treatment failure in South Africa (a clinical outcome, not trial withdrawal). — [PMC4608026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4608026/). **[Descriptive]**
- A schizophrenia MEMS study (6-month post-discharge) characterized adherence patterns, but dropout prediction was not its aim. — [PubMed 28663027](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28663027/).
- RTSM/IRT vendor documentation describes systems capturing discontinuation events and dropout trends for supply forecasting, and a USPTO patent (AstraZeneca-style LSTM) describes predicting discontinuation reason and timing, but neither is peer-reviewed evidence. — [Medidata RTSM explainer](https://www.medidata.com/en/life-science-resources/medidata-blog/what-is-rtsm/); [Signant Health RTSM blog](https://signanthealth.com/resources/blog/clinical-trial-supply-chain-management-rtsm-irt); [USPTO patent 12299564, "Pattern discovery, prediction and causal effect estimation in treatment discontinuation"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12299564). **[Grey literature]**

### Inferences
- The absence of published IRT-dispensing-based dropout work is itself a finding worth stating: the claim "dispensing/return data can flag withdrawal early" is plausible by analogy (HIV dispensing frequency; in-trial non-adherence events) but is currently unvalidated in IMP trials. A talk should label it as a hypothesis the supply-chain community is uniquely positioned to test, not an established capability.
- Because pill-count returns are inflated and often "suspiciously perfect", a return-based flag should probably key on missing or late returns and dispensing gaps (events that did not happen) rather than on counted adherence percentage.
- Dispensing gaps are already a strong LTFU feature in routine HIV care because dispensing = a visit; in a trial, the same signal is largely collinear with missed visits, so its incremental value beyond visit data is unknown.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper found that reports AUC, sensitivity or lead time for an IRT/RTSM- or medication-return-derived withdrawal flag.
- No published study found linking MEMS-cap adherence trajectories to subsequent trial withdrawal with a predictive metric.

## Key question 4: Sample size, generalizability, and rule-based flags vs models in small trials

### Takeaway
Prediction-model methodology (Riley et al. 2019) rejects the "10 events per variable" rule of thumb in favour of criteria that typically require far more events than a 300-patient trial with a 15-20% dropout rate (roughly 45-60 events) can supply for anything beyond a handful of predictors; external validation needs ≥100, ideally ≥200, events. Authors of dropout models repeatedly report single-centre or single-dataset limits. For small trials, the literature supports transparent rule-based flags built on observed in-trial behaviour, with any model presented as a pooled, cross-trial asset rather than trial-by-trial.

### Cited Findings
- Riley RD, Snell KIE, Ensor J, Burke DL, Harrell FE, Moons KGM, Collins GS (2019), "Minimum sample size for developing a multivariable prediction model: PART II - binary and time-to-event outcomes", Statistics in Medicine 38(7):1276-1296, DOI 10.1002/sim.7992, PubMed 30357870. Proposes criteria on participants (n), events (E) and candidate parameters (p) to limit overfitting and estimate overall risk precisely; states there is no evidence supporting events-per-predictor rules of thumb. Worked examples show required EPP ranging from 4.8 (a Chagas diagnostic model) to 23 (a recurrent VTE prognostic model), "which reinforces why rules of thumb (eg, 10 EPP) should be avoided". — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/sim.7992); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30357870/). **[Methodological standard]**
- Companion Part I (continuous outcomes): Riley et al. 2019, Statistics in Medicine, DOI 10.1002/sim.7993. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/sim.7993).
- External validation guidance: simulation studies indicate a minimum of 100 events and 100 non-events, with ≥200 events preferred, for a validation study; RCT populations are "often healthier" and measure predictors differently from routine practice, limiting transportability. — [Assessing discriminative performance at external validation of clinical prediction models, PLOS One 2016, PMC4755533](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4755533/); [External validation of prognostic models: what, why, how, when and where?, Clinical Kidney Journal 2021](https://academic.oup.com/ckj/article/14/1/49/6000246); [Debray et al. 2015, J Clin Epidemiol, framework for external validation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25179855/). **[Methodological]**
- Three types of generalizability (temporal, geographical, domain) and the internal vs external validation distinction (reproducibility vs transportability). — [Perspectives on validation of clinical predictive algorithms, npj Digital Medicine 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-023-00832-9); [Don't be misled: 3 misconceptions about external validation, J Clin Epidemiol 2024](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(24)00142-2/fulltext).
- Authors' own generalizability caveats in the dropout literature: JAMIA Open HIV model, "single urban healthcare center; limited generalizability" — [PMC12289141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12289141/); Wheeler 2022, "small sample size with few withdrawal events" (17 events) — [PMC9716754](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9716754/); PTSD IPD, only 3 trials with participant-level data — [PMC12120863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12120863/); OncoBERT, Brier 0.20 and 84% of ED cases missed — [PMC13427660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13427660/); NSABP B-35 AUC CIs as wide as 0.57-0.95 with a 70/30 split of one trial — [PMC10618646](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618646/); TrialBench flags selection bias toward trials with complete results — [arXiv 2407.00631](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00631v2).
- Dropout definitions and reason reporting are inconsistent across trials, which undermines pooling: Wright et al. 2025 call for "standardized protocols for defining and reporting dropout in RCTs" — [PMC12120863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12120863/); antipsychotic IPD meta-analysis notes AE-related dropouts are misclassified — [PubMed 42139983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42139983/). Cochrane (Gillies 2021) finds retention-strategy evidence itself is low quality — [Cochrane](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.MR000032.pub3/full/po).
- Missing-consecutive-visits is "widely accepted as an indicator of high drop-out risk and has been used as a proxy for drop-out" in trial analyses (Look AHEAD). — [Fitzpatrick et al. 2014, Obesity](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/oby.20613). **[Supports simple rule-based flags]**

### Inferences
- Arithmetic for the talk (derived, not cited): a 300-patient trial at the Tufts 19% average yields about 57 dropouts. Under the ranges reported in Riley's worked examples (roughly 5-23 events per parameter), that supports on the order of 3 to 10 candidate parameters at most, and leaves nothing for external validation (which wants ≥100 events). A trial of a few hundred patients therefore cannot both build and validate its own dropout model.
- The published models that "work" are either pooled across many trials (Medidata AD, AstraZeneca respiratory, TrialBench) or are large single trials (A4 n = 1,169; B-35 pooled PROs; HIV cohorts of 7,000+). This is the empirical basis for saying models are a portfolio/platform asset, while individual small trials should run rule-based flags.
- Rule-based flags have three advantages the literature implicitly supports: they use the predictors with the largest and most consistent effects (missed visits, non-adherence events, AE onset); they need no events to train; and they are auditable, which matters when the action is a site call rather than a clinical decision. Their cost is unquantified precision; the Medidata result (PR-AUC < 0.24) suggests even models struggle here, so rule-based false-alarm rates should be expected to be high and framed as "prompts for a conversation".
- Generalizability across trials is the central unresolved problem: predictor sets shift by therapeutic area, dropout definitions differ, and no external validation across sponsors is published. Any cross-trial model claim should be labelled as unvalidated.

### Gaps
- No paper found that directly compares a rule-based retention flag against a fitted model within the same trial(s).
- No published minimum-sample-size calculation applied specifically to trial-dropout prediction was found; the arithmetic above is an inference from Riley's general framework.
- Lead-time and cost-benefit (interventions triggered per true dropout prevented) are unreported across all retrieved model papers.

## Key question 5: Verifying the Tufts CSDD dropout figure (15.3% to 19.1%, 2012 to 2019)

### Takeaway
The draft's citation is consistent with the sources found: Ken Getz (Tufts CSDD) reported in Applied Clinical Trials Online that Tufts CSDD Impact Report data show late-phase global dropout rising from 15.3% in 2012 to 19.1% in 2019 (a 25% relative increase), with CNS 19.2% to 25.9% and oncology 18.2% to 19.3%. The exact ACT issue date and the Impact Report volume/number could not be confirmed because the ACT page blocked retrieval.

### Cited Findings
- "the overall average dropout rate across all studies and therapeutic areas increased 25%, from a rate of 15.3% up to 19.1% during a seven-year time period"; author Ken Getz, MBA, Deputy Director and Research Professor, Tufts CSDD. — [Getz, "Can Recruitment and Retention Get Any Worse?", Applied Clinical Trials Online](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/can-recruitment-and-retention-get-any-worse) (quoted in search index; page itself returned HTTP 403). **[Industry report]**
- "the trial dropout rate rose to 19.1% in late phase trials globally in 2019 from 15.3% in 2012 ... CNS trial dropout rates grew to 25.9% in 2019 from 19.2% in 2012, and oncology trial rates grew to 19.3% from 18.2%. Vaccine trials experienced a 12.3% dropout rate in 2019. Cardiovascular and rare disease trials fared best, with rates of 7% and 6.5%." attributed to the Tufts CSDD Impact Report. — [Replior blog, "The Dreaded Patient Drop Out"](https://www.replior.com/resources/all/blog/the-dreaded-patient-drop-out/). **[Secondary source, consistent with the ACT article]**
- A different Tufts CSDD series (2010-2020) reports a 105.1% relative increase in dropout rates; not the same denominator. — [Clinical Trial Vanguard](https://www.clinicaltrialvanguard.com/conference-coverage/tufts-csdd-new-insights-on-the-clinical-trial-industry/).
- A frequently repeated industry figure that "approximately 30% of those who initially agree to participate subsequently withdraw" appears without a primary source in the Clinical Leader DCT piece. — [Clinical Leader](https://www.clinicalleader.com/doc/tufts-finds-substantial-net-benefits-to-using-decentralized-trials-0001). **[Unsourced; avoid]**

### Inferences
- Recommended slide citation: Getz K. "Can Recruitment and Retention Get Any Worse?" Applied Clinical Trials Online (Tufts CSDD Impact Report data, late-phase global trials, 2012-2019). Add "late-phase" and "global average" qualifiers; the 19.1% is not a per-trial expectation, and therapeutic-area rates range from 6.5% to 25.9%.
- The 105.1% figure should not be presented alongside 15.3% to 19.1% without explaining they are different measures over different windows.

### Gaps
- Exact publication date of the ACT article and the Impact Report volume/issue (Tufts CSDD Impact Reports are bimonthly, subscription-only) were not confirmed; the search-index snippet and secondary source agree on the numbers but not on the report citation. Recommend checking the ACT byline date directly before printing.
- No newer (2021-2026) Tufts CSDD average dropout figure was found in the retrieved sources.
