# Nobody Has Yet Mined Dispensing Data for Dropout

Across the peer-reviewed literature, vendor products, conference agendas and sponsor case studies reviewed here, **no published study, product or presentation uses IRT/RTSM dispensing, kit-return or shipment data as an input to a patient-level dropout prediction**; every existing "dropout radar" runs on EDC, patient-reported outcomes, EHR notes, app telemetry or baseline questionnaires. The predictors that do replicate are behavioural and observable inside the trial: missed or rescheduled visits, incomplete procedures, early non-adherence and adverse events, with a consistent 7-to-14-day early window in digital and dosing data, while baseline demographics are weak and inconsistent across therapeutic areas. Published participant-level models cluster at **AUC 0.65-0.80**, almost all with internal validation only, and the most honest cross-industry result (Medidata/AbbVie, 8,103 AD patients) reports **ROC-AUC of about 0.60 and PR-AUC below 0.24**, meaning flags will be noisy. The eClinical stack already computes retention indicators, but as lagging, site-level KRIs and trial-level QTLs; IRT vendors treat dropout as a supply-forecast input rather than something to predict per patient; and the 2026 Informa IRT & RTSM agenda has five integration sessions that stop at "reconciliation" and one session, yours, that mentions dropout at all. For an audience running trials of a few hundred patients, the arithmetic is unforgiving: roughly 57 dropouts in a 300-patient trial supports a handful of rule-based flags, not a fitted and validated model. The defensible talk positions dispensing-derived flags as a plausible, cheap, auditable leading indicator by analogy to HIV dispensing data and in-trial non-adherence, states plainly that it is unvalidated in IMP trials, and proposes the supply-chain community as the group uniquely placed to test it.

## Behavioural in-trial signals outpredict baseline demographics everywhere they are tested

The headline attrition trend is real and citable. Tufts CSDD Impact Report data, as reported by Ken Getz, show the average late-phase global dropout rate rising from **15.3% in 2012 to 19.1% in 2019**, with CNS moving from 19.2% to 25.9%, oncology from 18.2% to 19.3%, vaccines at 12.3%, and cardiovascular and rare disease best at 7% and 6.5% ([Getz, Applied Clinical Trials](https://www.appliedclinicaltrialsonline.com/view/can-recruitment-and-retention-get-any-worse); confirmed via [Replior](https://www.replior.com/resources/all/blog/the-dreaded-patient-drop-out/)). A separate Tufts series presented by Getz at SCOPE 2024 reports dropout rates up **105.1% between 2010 and 2020** alongside a 36.9% rise in enrollment duration and 40.6% more procedures per protocol ([Clinical Trial Vanguard](https://www.clinicaltrialvanguard.com/conference-coverage/tufts-csdd-new-insights-on-the-clinical-trial-industry/)). These are different measures over different windows and should not appear on one slide as a single series; the therapeutic-area spread from 6.5% to 25.9% also means 19.1% is not a per-trial expectation.

What predicts which patient leaves is a different question, and the answer is consistent: things the patient does after enrollment. The cleanest small-trial evidence is Wheeler et al. (2022), a prospective urology study of 123 participants in which **every one of the 17 withdrawals had at least one prior non-adherence event** (an incomplete procedure or scheduling problem) versus 48.1% of retained participants; repeated non-adherence was 88.2% versus 16.9%, and the count of non-adherence events was the only independent predictor at **OR 12.5 (95% CI 3.5-43.1)**, while socioeconomic index, age, race, BMI and comorbidity predicted nothing ([Trials, PMC9716754](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9716754/)). The Look AHEAD investigators treat missed consecutive data-collection visits as an accepted proxy for dropout risk ([Fitzpatrick et al. 2014, Obesity](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/oby.20613)). In a five-week light-therapy RCT, completing under 80% of the prescribed week-one dose carried a **5.85-fold higher dropout risk** independent of age, sex, arm and adverse events ([PMC9397075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9397075/)). Adverse events and lack of benefit sit behind these behaviours: in antipsychotic trials, active-arm dropout was 48.1% in placebo-controlled versus 28.3% in active-control designs, and placebo-arm dropout reached 60.2% ([Kemmler 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16330718/)); in antidepressant trials, all-cause dropout rose about 4% for every 10-point increase in the probability of placebo assignment ([Furukawa 2018](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/47/5/1454/5034083)). Placebo assignment is a genuine arm-level risk but unusable as a per-patient flag in a blinded trial; it argues for trial-level overage planning instead.

Baseline factors are weaker and shift by therapeutic area. The A4 preclinical Alzheimer's trial (n = 1,169, 29% discontinued over a median 759 days) found only baseline anxiety (OR 1.07 per STAI point) and age (OR 1.06 per year); sex, race, cognition and amyloid or tau PET were unrelated ([Raman 2025, PMC11712999](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11712999/)). An IPD analysis of PTSD pharmacotherapy trials found no effect of age, marital status, severity or comorbidity ([Wright 2025, PMC12120863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12120863/)). Consent comprehension and visit stress matter: early dropouts were twice as likely to find the consent form hard to understand (35% vs 16%) and to describe site visits as stressful (38% vs 16%) ([Fogel 2018, PMC6092479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6092479/)). Travel burden is measured but its link to dropout in IMP trials is not: cancer trial participants travel a median 25.8 miles one way, with lower-income patients at 58.3 miles ([PubMed 29700209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29700209/)), and IQVIA's "10% retention loss per additional 30 miles" appears in a blog without a primary source ([IQVIA](https://www.iqvia.com/blogs/2021/03/can-decentralized-clinical-trials-solve-attrition)). No peer-reviewed hazard ratio for distance-to-site as a dropout predictor in IMP trials was found. The implication for a supply-chain audience is that the useful predictor set is exactly the event layer operational systems already timestamp, and that any universal flag list is a starting hypothesis rather than a validated scorecard.

Three lessons fall out of the table. First, the only models above 0.85 come from an EHR population with heavy class-imbalance handling (Haiti), a lifestyle app with a generous four-week dropout definition (LIVA), or a vendor abstract of 109 patients; **0.7 to 0.8 is the realistic ceiling** for interventional-trial data today. Second, precision collapses: Medidata's PR-AUC under 0.24 and OncoBERT missing 84% of early-discontinuation cases at its operating point mean an early-warning system will generate many false alarms unless the base rate is high. Third, transfer is unproven: the Brighten elastic net lost half its F1 moving between two cohorts of the same platform, and TrialBench's trial-level benchmark on 62,058 registry trials reaches only ROC-AUC 0.73 for high-versus-low dropout classification while predicting nothing about which enrolled patient leaves ([arXiv 2407.00631](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00631v2)). AstraZeneca's respiratory attrition model on 3,175 cohorts and one million patients likewise predicts a cohort's dropout percentage (test RMSE 6.64 points), with trial duration, treatment duration, indication and adverse-event count as the top drivers ([Hutchison 2021 preprint](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1101/2021.11.12.21266277?fields=title,authors,year,abstract,venue); [code](https://github.com/AstraZeneca/CTELC-Patient-Attrition-Model)).

The Medidata/AbbVie poster deserves a close read because it is the direct precedent for what you are proposing. Its Cox models used **a site-operational proxy derived from an eClinical system**, clinical staff size estimated from average Rave user counts per site (HR 1.01, p = .02), alongside low surrounding population density (HR 10.50), age (HR 1.07), anxiety history (HR 1.44) and an anxiety adverse event during the trial (HR 1.54), and concluded that "a limited set of patient and site features can be used to estimate patient dropout risk within 3, 6, and 12 months of randomization" ([poster PDF](https://www.medidata.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/AAIC-2021-Identifying-Predictive-Factors-of-Patient-Dropout-in-Alzheimers-Disease-Clinical-Trials.pdf)). A scientific congress accepted an EDC-login artefact as a dropout predictor; IRT dispensing events are at least as defensible an operational feature. What no paper in this set reports is lead time. NSABP B-35 uses a six-month horizon, Medidata three, six and twelve months, OncoBERT sixty days from consent, and none states the median gap between a flag firing and the withdrawal. That absence is a white space the talk can name.

## Digital and dosing data open a 7-to-14-day window that IRT could mirror

The eCOA, wearable and adherence-technology literature is where lead time actually shows up, and it converges on the first two weeks. AiCure's peer-reviewed analysis of two Phase II CNS trials (n = 235 schizophrenia plus an attenuated-psychosis cohort) found that app-confirmed dosing in the first two weeks predicted adherence for the rest of the trial, adherence in the latest four weeks predicted premature dropout, and a 0.6 adherence cutoff over a **14-day window flagged 19% of participants with 96% subsequent confirmation** of non-adherence; the eCRF recorded 78% compliance where the app showed 26% in one cohort ([CNS Spectrums](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cns-spectrums/article/aibased-adherence-prediction-for-patients-leveraging-a-mobile-application-to-improve-clinical-trials/DC2E0B8A27D2AD5BD8EBED6C85151264)). The LIVA platform found that **71.8% of dropouts cut activity by more than half in their final two weeks**, chose a two-week inactivity gap as a top feature, and defined dropout as four idle weeks because fewer than 5% re-engage after that ([JMIR 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6753691/)). In the Cedars-Sinai EHR-connected wearable cohort of 8,616 patients, first-week step volume predicted long-term disengagement (HR 0.72-0.79 for higher-step groups) ([JMIR 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12489408/)). Pratap's pooled analysis of eight remote studies with over 100,000 participants shows why the window is so short in app-only research: **median retention was 5.5 days**, more than half of participants quit within the first week, and clinician referral (+40 days median retention) and compensation (+22 days) were the largest levers ([npj Digital Medicine 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7026051/)).

Two subtler findings matter for signal design. RADAR-MDD (614 depression participants over up to two years) showed that passive data outlasts active data: **44.6% of those who stopped completing surveys kept sharing Fitbit data for an average of 42 more weeks**, and the least-engaged group answered notifications 3.8 hours slower ([npj Digital Medicine 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-023-00749-3)). Missed active tasks fire first; passive decline is closer to formal withdrawal; and response latency is a cheap, already-timestamped signal almost nobody uses. The dispensing analogue is direct: a late or missed dispensing visit is the "active" event, a kit not returned with no subsequent dispensation is the "passive" confirmation. COVID-RED (17,825 participants) is the one published example of a signal-triggered intervention in a trial: the team used bracelet compliance of 30-70% as the high-risk band, called the flagged stratum, and saw **dropout of 11.4% among called participants versus 20.3% in the same stratum before the intervention**, non-randomised ([PMC12514562](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12514562/)).

The intervention evidence sets the ceiling for what "prevent" can mean. The Cochrane review of 81 retention trials with over 100,000 participants found only monetary incentives with any confidence (RR 1.20, 95% CI 1.06-1.36, low certainty); electronic reminders (RR 1.01), SMS prompts (1.03), telephone follow-up (1.04) and personalised prompts (0.97) did nothing, and adding a diary slightly hurt retention (RR 0.97, moderate certainty); none of the comparisons reached high-certainty evidence ([Gillies 2021, PMC8092429](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8092429/)). Against that, targeted human contact triggered by a signal shows the strongest real-world effects: the All of Us telephone-appointment arm (n = 6,253) had adjusted OR 2.01 (95% CI 1.81-2.23) for survey completion while postal contact alone did nothing ([PLOS One 2024](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0308995)). The distinction between blanket nudges (no effect) and signal-triggered outreach (consistent non-randomised effect) is the key slide, with the caveat that no RCT has tested signal-triggered outreach on withdrawal. Vendor alerting is almost universally threshold-based (compliance below X%) rather than trajectory-based, and the 88-89% retention figures from ObvioHealth's six-study internal analysis (752 enrolled) and Datacubed's 36-patient case are single-arm, short, BYOD studies ([ObvioHealth](https://www.obviohealth.com/resources/meta-analysis-epro-compliance-and-retention-for-patients-using-their-own-smartphones-in-fully-decentralized-clinical-trials); [Datacubed](https://www.datacubed.com/retention-and-compliance-case-study/)); present them as "what good looks like", not as proof that alerts cause retention.

## The eClinical stack aggregates to sites and treats dropout as a forecast input

RBQM is where retention is already monitored, and its design explains why no per-patient score exists. TransCelerate's library of more than 140 risk indicators includes a "Subject discontinuation" category (ratio of discontinued to randomized, reasons for discontinuation) and an "Investigational Product" category (accountability, dosing, compliance), but it states that indicators computed at the patient level trigger actions "predominantly on the site level" ([TransCelerate](https://www.transceleratebiopharmainc.com/rbminteractiveguide/best-practices-for-implementation/risk-indicators-and-thresholds/)). CluePoints' nine standard KRIs include early termination rate (early-terminated patients divided by post-randomization visits, flagged when the site's statistical score exceeds 1.3), missed assessment rate and off-schedule visit rate; its outcomes study of **212 studies, 1,676 sites and 23 sponsors** found 82.9% of KRI signals improved after action, but enrollment and retention KRIs improved far less than data-quality KRIs, with off-schedule visit rate improving in only 23.1% of cases ([de Viron 2022, PMC9589525](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9589525/)). Retention signals are harder to remediate once they fire because they are lagging: the patient has already left. The historical QTL benchmarks from seven TransCelerate members and 294 trials give useful base rates for a slide, with mean premature drug discontinuation of **16.03% (median 10.70%)**, withdrawal of consent 3.95%, and lost to follow-up 1.78% ([Makowski 2021, PMC8492573](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8492573/)), and a TIRS worked example sets a 12% premature-discontinuation QTL for a 300-participant Phase III with a 4% historical rate ([PMC10850247](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10850247/)). ICH E6(R3) now requires sponsors to set quality tolerance limits on critical-to-quality factors, lists "reviewing and reporting the participant recruitment and retention rates" as a monitor activity, and obliges investigators to "make a reasonable effort to ascertain the reason(s)" for withdrawal ([ICH E6(R3) final, Jan 2025](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH_E6(R3)_Step4_FinalGuideline_2025_0106.pdf)). That investigator duty is the regulatory hook: surfacing at-risk participants before withdrawal is what makes the conversation possible.

Within IRT and RTSM, the arrow runs the other way. Signant's Bill Byrom describes RTSM systems aggregating "historical enrollment patterns, screen failure rates, patient dropout trends, and site activation timelines to generate accurate supply forecasts" ([Signant](https://signanthealth.com/resources/blog/clinical-trial-supply-chain-management-rtsm-irt)); N-SIDE simulates each patient's "recruitment, screening, treatment, titration, dropout, and drug dispensing" and updates as "dropout rate per visit and treatment arm change" ([N-SIDE](https://www.n-side.com/en/insights/ai-in-clinical-supply-chain-episode-2/)). Suvoda, YPrime, Medidata, IQVIA, 4G, Almac, Veeva and Calyx all market real-time subject status, visit scheduling and drug-accountability reconciliation, and Suvoda's Sofia assistant surfaces "enrollment velocity, site performance trends, and supply risk" ([Suvoda](https://www.suvoda.com/insights/all-news/new-ai-assistant-simplify-clinical-trial-management)), but none markets a patient-level retention score from dispensing. The closest hooks are Endpoint's PULSE "NUDGE alerts" for compliance feedback (brochure blocked; wording unverified) ([Endpoint](https://info.endpointclinical.com/pulse-brochure)) and ScienceSoft's custom-build description of flagging discrepancies between IRT dispensing and eCOA-reported consumption ([ScienceSoft](https://www.scnsoft.com/healthcare/clinical-trials/irt)). The only vendor language claiming to "identify risk factors for dropout early" is a DDi blog with no method or evidence ([DDi](https://www.ddismart.com/blog/how-ai-advanced-analytics-transforming-rtsm-irt-systems/)). Among the wider vendor field, Cognivia Signal (launched October 2025) claims 93% accuracy on a T1D validation with no n or confusion matrix and uses questionnaire inputs ([Clinical Trials Arena](https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/cognivias-ai-software-predicts-probability-of-trial-patient-dropping-out/)), Ripple Science claims 87.5% accuracy from engagement-platform signals ([Ripple](https://www.ripplescience.com/automation-and-predictive-analytics-as-tools-to-improve-retention-predicting-and-avoiding-study-dropout/)), and Parexel, Syneos, PPD TrueCast, Fortrea and IQVIA market enrollment forecasting and site selection, not dropout ([Parexel](https://www.parexel.com/solutions/parexelai/ai-in-clinical-development); [PPD](https://www.ppd.com/blog/make-or-break-clinical-trial-solutions/)). With a 20-30% base rate, "accuracy" without precision and recall is close to meaningless, and the talk should say so.

What IRT reliably records, and what it does not, determines which dispensing signals are trustworthy. Standard SAPs compute compliance as tablets dispensed minus returned over tablets prescribed, and encode the missing-return rule that matters most here: "if a kit is not returned and no kit is subsequently dispensed, then the non-returned kit is assumed to not have been taken" ([NCT04076059 SAP](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/59/NCT04076059/SAP_001.pdf)). The quantity layer is unreliable: MEMS adherence was 91.6% versus 96.1% by pill count in 228 hypertensive patients, with only 61% agreement on adherent/non-adherent classification ([van Onzenoort 2010](https://academic.oup.com/ajh/article/23/2/149/2281931)); in two HSV-2 trials of 1,305 and 3,277 participants, 11-19% of visits showed over-adherence above 105%, which correlated with reduced antiviral effect and looked like pill dumping ([Baisley 2013, PMC3812335](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3812335/)). The event layer is objective: dispensation happened or did not, on a date, kit returned or not, subsequent dispensation or none. A return-based flag should therefore key on events that did not happen (no dispensation at the expected visit, dispensation outside window, kit not returned with no subsequent dispensation, implausible counts above 105% or below 50%) rather than on a computed compliance percentage. The indirect evidence that this works is the Haiti HIV model, where ART dispensation patterns and visit frequency were the strongest LTFU features ([IJPH 2026](https://www.ssph-journal.org/journals/international-journal-of-public-health/articles/10.3389/ijph.2026.1609496/full)), with the caveat that in routine care dispensing is the visit, so its incremental value beyond visit data in a trial is unknown.

## Sponsors built control towers and one dropout model, but never joined the two

The industry has solved the plumbing without adding the analytics layer. IRT-EDC integration is standard and is sold on reconciliation and eligibility gating ([IntuitionLabs](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/rtsm-edc-integration-clinical-trials)); Medidata's Syneos case reports eliminated reconciliation with no retention metric ([Medidata](https://www.medidata.com/en/life-science-resources/medidata-blog/bringing-data-together-without-complicated-integrations-rave-rtsm/)). Eli Lilly is the only top-15 sponsor found with a named clinical supply "control tower", presented by Jill Riley and Mohit Gupta at the AWS Life Sciences Symposium on 14 April 2026, framed as "a unified, single pane of glass" moving "from descriptive to predictive and prescriptive operations" with the patient benefit stated as supply reliability, and no mention of retention ([AWS symposium](https://aws.amazon.com/health/aws-life-sciences-symposium-2026/); [Gupta LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mohit-gupta-75b1b7167_elililly-dan-aws-activity-7450313689167060993-j6Hb)). Anonymised control-tower case studies measure success in stockouts, write-offs and disruptions: Syren's global-pharma client with 65+ trials claims 10% fewer stockouts and $2M-$4.6M/year of write-offs avoided ([Syren](https://syrencloud.com/case-studies/building-an-intelligent-clinical-trial-control-tower/)); ZS claims a 30% reduction in supply disruptions ([ZS](https://www.zs.com/insights/digital-command-center-clinical-supply-chain-solution)). None reports a patient-level KPI, which is itself citable.

The supply-failure-to-dropout link the talk rests on is asserted everywhere and measured nowhere. Tom Wells writes that "missed deliveries lead to missed doses, which erode confidence and increase dropout risk" ([Clinical Leader, Feb 2026](https://www.clinicalleader.com/doc/the-key-role-of-clinical-supply-teams-in-patient-centricity-efforts-0001)); Bob Lozito that "stockouts increase patient dropouts" ([Clinical Supply Leader, Apr 2026](https://www.clinicalsupplyleader.com/doc/the-hidden-supply-chain-risks-that-can-derail-small-biotech-trials-0001)); neither has data. The quantified items are indirect: a National Academies report notes one oncology trial enrolled under 60% of target because of a comparator shortage ([NBK583734](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK583734/)); an N-SIDE model flagged 425 patients at risk of missed doses within two months ([N-SIDE](https://lifesciences.n-side.com/blog/how-to-reduce-the-risk-of-a-drug-shortage-in-your-clinical-trial)); and the ASCOLT trial documented COVID-era "disruption of drug supply and increased loss to follow-up" with sites switching to mailed kits ([Segelov 2020, PMC7193776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7193776/)). Direct-to-patient retention claims are the same shape. The Tufts CSDD survey in DIA Global Forum (November 2025; 301 participants, 160 sites) found **90% of participants with DTP experience said it helped them stay enrolled** and 60% of sites said it improved completion, but it measured perception, not dropout ([DIA Global Forum](https://globalforum.diaglobal.org/issue/november-2025/direct-to-patient-dtp-shipments-of-investigational-treatments-in-clinical-trials-participant-and-site-receptivity/)). Science 37's 82-98% retention figures are single-arm vendor cases with unnamed sponsors ([Science 37](https://www.science37.com/resources/case-studies)), and the Science 37/Catalent DTP programme of 17 studies and 6,400 shipments reports no retention outcome at all ([SCRS](https://myscrs.org/resources/redefining-access-in-clinical-trials/)). No controlled comparison of retention with and without DTP exists, and no DTP source treats the shipment record itself (delivery confirmation, missed delivery, reschedule) as a retention signal.

On the conference circuit the space is empty. On the confirmed 2026 Informa IRT & RTSM agenda, "Beyond Dispensing" (Day 2, Track 2, 2:00-2:30pm, 7 October) is **the only session whose description mentions dropout or retention** ([Agenda Day 2](https://informaconnect.com/irt-rtsm/agenda/2/)). Running in parallel, "Connected by Design" (Monte Rosa, Alumis) frames IRT-EDC integration as reducing reconciliation burden; on Day 1, "From System to Strategy" (4G Clinical, J&J) and "Breaking the Silos" (Oracle, Merck, Biohaven) cover integration architecture and cross-study data strategy; "Better Together" (GSK, Regeneron, BioMarin) covers organisational alignment; and "Beyond the Hype" (BMS, GSK) covers AI for forecasting ([Agenda Day 1](https://informaconnect.com/irt-rtsm/agenda/)). Nothing on the agenda contradicts the framing that once IRT is joined to EDC and CTMS, dispensing cadence becomes a retention signal, and the talk can position itself as the "so what" of those five sessions. The GCSG circuit has asserted DTP-improves-retention since at least its 2020 bootcamp without study-level numbers ([GCSG](https://mygcsg.com/session/exploring-direct-to-patient-clinical-trial-models/)), CTS East Coast 2026 runs the arrow the reverse way (using eConsent and remote-monitoring data to trigger resupply) ([Arena CTS](https://www.arena-international.com/event/ctseastcoast/)), and TransCelerate and CTTI treat retention as a patient-experience and planning topic, never as a dispensing-derived signal ([TransCelerate](https://www.transceleratebiopharmainc.com/initiatives/patient-experience/); [CTTI](https://ctti-clinicaltrials.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/CTTI_Recruitment_Recs.pdf)). Informa's 2019-2025 agendas could not be retrieved (archive URLs 404), so "nobody has presented this at IRT & RTSM" should be phrased as "not on any agenda I could find" until you check the Wayback Machine.

## A 300-patient trial can run flags but cannot build or validate a model

The arithmetic is the honest core of the talk. Riley et al. (2019) reject the "10 events per variable" rule of thumb and show that the events needed per candidate parameter ranged from 4.8 to 23 across worked examples ([Statistics in Medicine](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/sim.7992)); external validation studies need at least 100 events and preferably 200 ([PLOS One 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4755533/)). A 300-patient trial at the Tufts 19% average yields about 57 dropouts, which under Riley's ranges supports on the order of three to ten candidate parameters and leaves nothing for validation. The models that "work" are pooled (Medidata's seven trials, AstraZeneca's 3,175 cohorts) or large (A4 at 1,169; HIV cohorts of 7,000+), and every author reports the same limits: single centre, few events, wide intervals. The audience should hear that **a trial of a few hundred patients cannot both build and validate its own dropout model**, so a model is a portfolio or platform asset, while the individual trial runs transparent rule-based flags on the predictors with the largest effects (missed or late dispensing visits, non-adherence events, adverse-event onset). Rule-based flags need no training events, are auditable, and are proportionate when the action is a site phone call rather than a clinical decision; their cost is an unquantified false-alarm rate, which the Medidata precision result says will be high. Framing flags as "prompts for a conversation" that satisfy the E6(R3) investigator duty is both accurate and defensible.

The table below separates what can be said to an expert audience from what cannot.

One further caveat belongs in the talk. In oncology and other progressive diseases, "early discontinuation" is dominated by disease progression (71.5% of withdrawals in a CRC survey) rather than disengagement ([PMC9096735](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9096735/)), so a dispensing-based flag will fire on clinical events it cannot prevent; the outcome definition (consent withdrawal and lost-to-follow-up, not all discontinuation) has to be fixed before any flag is judged. Dropout definitions are also inconsistent across trials, which is why Wright et al. call for standardised reporting ([PMC12120863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12120863/)) and why any cross-study analysis of IRT data will first have to reconcile what "dropout" means in each protocol.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the talk's central claim from a capability to a hypothesis with unusually good priors. Every ingredient exists in isolation: behavioural predictors with large effects, a documented 7-to-14-day early window in dosing and engagement data, a scientific-congress precedent for operational eClinical features in a dropout model, an E6(R3) duty to learn withdrawal reasons, an RBQM layer that already ingests visit and compliance data, and an IRT event layer that timestamps exactly the "things that did not happen" the predictors point to. What is missing is the one study that joins them, and the supply-chain community owns the data needed to run it. The most credible version of "Beyond Dispensing" therefore ends not with a product but with a protocol: a definition of the outcome, four or five event-based flags, a per-study threshold with a feedback loop, a site-call intervention, and lead time and precision as the two metrics nobody has yet reported.

The second implication is about ambition. The industry's own numbers say the honest ceiling for a patient-level model is AUC 0.7-0.8 with many false alarms, that no model has transferred across cohorts, and that a few-hundred-patient trial cannot validate anything. That is not a weakness to hide from an expert audience; it is the reason a cheap, auditable, rule-based dispensing radar is the right first move and a pooled cross-sponsor model is the right second one. Saying so will read as more informed than any vendor's 93%.
